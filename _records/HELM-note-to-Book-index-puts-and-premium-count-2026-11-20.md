# Index-fund puts against single-stock puts, and one number for the market read

*Reply from the options desk (HELM) to the Book of Books, carried by Russ. 20 November 2026. Findings and recommendations for Russ to decide. Nothing has changed at the desk and nothing has been built.*

## The short answer

1. **Not "rather than". Index funds as the core, single stocks around them, is the right course, and the desk's rules currently prevent it.** The rules were written for single companies. Applied to index funds, they keep the S&P fund off the list on most days and make it too big to place on the rest. Russ should decide whether that is what he wants. I don't think it was intended.
2. **The case for index puts is safety, not income.** Over ten years they took a third as many large losses and had no company gaps or reports. The evidence does not show they earn more on the cash, and at quoted prices they pay about half what the desk's usual stock puts pay.
3. **When a put is sold matters more than what it is sold on.** The gap between selling in a quiet market and selling after a fall is several times the gap between funds and stocks.
4. **The one number:** this morning 69 of the desk's 102 sellable names had rich premium by the desk's measure. I recommend adding it to the daily file. Details in part 2.

## 1. Index funds or single stocks

### What the ten-year study says

The study's puts were split into the four big index funds (S&P, Nasdaq 100, Russell 2000, Dow), the sector funds, and single stocks. Same put throughout: about six weeks out, 0.27 delta, held to expiry, weekly from November 2017 to October 2026. Figures are per put, as a share of the cash set aside.

| | Premium taken in | Kept after losses, per year | Traded below the strike at some point | Finished below the strike | Large losses (over 10% of the cash) | A bad result (1 in 20) | Worst |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S&P fund, priced off the fear gauge | 1.2% | 5.0% | 32% | 13% | 1.3% | −3.1% | −23% |
| Four big index funds | 1.2% | 2.9% | 41% | 18% | 1.9% | −4.5% | −32% |
| Sector funds | 1.4% | 3.3% | 43% | 20% | 2.1% | −4.8% | −45% |
| Single stocks (108 names) | 2.5% | 5.4% | 44% | 23% | 6.1% | −9.0% | −78% |

*"Kept per year" leaves out interest on the cash, which is the same for all. Both halves of the decade agree: index funds 2.8% and 3.0%, single stocks 4.8% and 5.8%.*

**Reading it**
- **Safety: index funds, clearly.** A third as many large losses, a bad result half as deep, and a worst case of −32% against −78%.
- **Tested about as often.** Roughly four puts in ten trade below the strike at some point in every group. An index put sits closer to the price because the index moves less.
- **Income: no winner can be called.** Single stocks show more, but two things bend the comparison in opposite directions:
  - The study had no option prices and priced every put off the name's own recent swings. Index options trade well above that. Repricing the S&P fund off the fear gauge, which is a real market price, nearly doubles its result, from 2.6% to 5.0% a year.
  - The single stocks are today's watchlist, picked knowing who thrived. That flatters them by an unknown amount.

**The worst stretches**

| | Index funds: average per put | Large losses | Single stocks: average per put | Large losses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Late 2018 | −1.6% | 1% | −1.6% | 14% |
| 2020 crash | −8.7% | 42% | −9.8% | 41% |
| 2022 | −0.4% | 4% | 0.0% | 10% |
| Spring 2025 | −2.6% | 8% | −2.0% | 18% |

This corrects the desk note of 20 November, which said index funds take "a fraction of the damage". **In a selloff an index-put book loses about as much in total per dollar as a stock-put book.** What differs is the shape: many small, even losses rather than a few crushing ones. In a true crash (2020) there was no difference at all.

**Quiet markets against after a fall** (kept per year)

| When sold | Index funds | Single stocks |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet (near-term fear well below longer-term) | 1.4% | 3.8% |
| Ordinary | 5.8% | 8.3% |
| After a 15% fall and a 5% bounce | 14.4% | 22.1% |
| All other times | 1.4% | 3.2% |

Both halves of the decade agree for both groups. Puts sold with near-term fear above longer-term fear gave opposite results in the two halves for both groups, so there is no reading there.

### What the real record says

Almost nothing. Of 89 real puts closed, 86 were on single stocks and 3 on funds: two on the energy fund and one on the semiconductor fund, all in the last three weeks. The desk has never sold a put on a broad index fund.

The stock record does show the single-stock pattern in real money: 68 winners in 86, and a net loss of about $4,700, because a few large losses outweighed many small wins. Much of that came from oversized trades early in the year, so it is a caution, not a measurement.

### How the rules steer the choice

Across 18 recorded put lists since late October:

| | Times checked | Turned away: premium not rich enough | Passed | Could be placed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S&P fund | 18 | 15 | 3 (all on 6 November) | 0 |
| Dow fund | 18 | 15 | 3 (all on 6 November) | 3, one contract |
| Nasdaq 100 fund | 18 | 7 | 11 | 0 |
| Russell 2000 fund | 18 | 18 | 0 | 0 |

Three rules do it:

1. **The "rich premium" bar.** The desk sells only when a name's implied volatility is in the upper half of its own past-year range. A stock gets there fairly often. An index sits low most of the year and spikes briefly. **Over ten years the S&P's fear gauge cleared that bar on one day in ten, and on fewer than one day in twenty in six of the ten years.**
2. **The one-name limit.** No name may take more than 20% of the sleeve, about $57,600. One S&P fund put secures $65,000 to $70,000 and one Nasdaq fund put about $64,000. Every time either passed the premium bar, it was sized at zero.
3. **The ranking.** The list ranks by yearly return. When index funds passed, the S&P and Dow funds ranked 31st to 35th and the Nasdaq fund usually 7th to 20th. Russ sees the top five.

**Does the premium bar earn its place on the index?** Not on this evidence. S&P puts sold on days that cleared the bar kept 8.0% a year against 4.7% on other days, but the two halves disagree (−0.9% against 5.2%, then 11.6% against 4.3%), there are only 45 such puts, and they had four times the large losses. The other nine days in ten still paid.

**Is it intended?** The funds were approved in October to be offered like any qualifying stock. The record shows no decision to keep index funds off the list. I read it as a side effect of single-company rules. Russ should say.

Separately, the sector funds are kept off mostly by wide markets: the health-care fund on all 18 lists, communications on 12, technology on 11.

### The practical side

| | Index fund put | Single-stock put |
|---|---|---|
| Ticket | S&P $65,000–70,000; Nasdaq about $64,000; Dow about $46,000; Russell about $24,000 | Median about $46,000 on the real record, and adjustable by contract count |
| Quoted yearly return on the lists | S&P and Dow 13–14%; Nasdaq 16–22% | Top five: median 39% |
| Option markets | S&P and Nasdaq spreads about 1%; Dow about 5% | Under 10% required; many fail |
| Reports | None | No sale within 10 days of one; 178 names turned away for it across the lists |
| Company gaps | None | 5 November: market −4.4%, Dell −13.8% |
| If assigned | 100 shares of the market: about $66,000 for the S&P fund, the easiest holding to keep, with the best market for selling calls against it | One company at a loss. Today: Google shares at about $273 against a $327 cost, and no call worth selling above cost |
| Market exposure added | About $19,000 for one S&P put | About the same per dollar of cash for a stock that moves with the market |

**Against the limits**
- **One name, 20%:** blocks one contract of the S&P or Nasdaq fund. The Dow fund fits one; the Russell fund fits two.
- **Sector, 30%:** the four index funds share one group, capped near $86,000. That allows one S&P put, or a Dow and a Russell put, and no more. "Index as the core" cannot exceed 30% of the sleeve as the rules stand.
- **Market exposure:** on 18 November the desk had about $22,500 of room under its ceiling. One S&P put would use nearly all of it. The same is true of any put that size.

### The middle course

**Workable in principle. Not workable under today's rules.** It would need three decisions from Russ:

1. **Judge index funds without the "rich premium" bar,** or with one suited to an index. The index's edge is there on ordinary days.
2. **Take broad index funds out of the one-name limit** and let their group limit govern. An index fund is not one company.
3. **Decide how big the core is.** Today's group cap is 30% of the sleeve. This is properly part of the Book's exposure work.

Single-stock rules would stay as they are, with the quality grade doing the "glad to own it" test.

**What it would change**
- **Less income at quoted prices.** Russ passed on an energy-fund put at 16% a year in October. Index puts quote at 13–22%.
- **Fewer, larger, calmer positions,** with no report dates to work around.
- **About the same loss in a broad selloff,** spread evenly.
- **Assignment leaves the market, not a company.**

### Recommendation

- **Adopt the middle course as the direction. Watch before any rule moves,** as the Book's market read is doing.
- **First step, display only:** show the best index-fund put each day beside the usual list, whether or not it clears the premium bar, with its quoted return and what one contract secures. After a month Russ has real quoted prices for both kinds side by side, which is the thing the study lacks.
- **Then Russ decides** on the three rule questions above.
- **Do not switch to index funds outright.** The income evidence doesn't support it and the tail evidence doesn't require it.

### Where the evidence is thin

- **No past option prices.** Every income figure here is modelled. Only the S&P fund could be repriced from a real market measure.
- **Survivors.** The single-stock results are flattered by an unknown amount.
- **No real index trades.** Three fund trades, none on a broad index.
- **Held to expiry.** The desk buys back at half the premium and closes at 21 days. Neither is in the study.
- **"Better paid for the risk" comes from published studies of option prices,** not from the desk's own numbers.
- **One decade,** without a long bear market.

### What would change the recommendation

- **A month of quoted prices showing index puts well under the return Russ needs.** Then the core is smaller, or the desk stays with stocks and tighter selection.
- **Evidence that the desk's quality-graded stocks, bought back at half, avoid most of the large losses.** That would weaken the safety case for index funds. The real record since October is too short to say.
- **The Book finding that the account already holds a great deal of index exposure elsewhere.** Then index puts add to a position Russ already has.
- **Option price history,** if Russ wants to buy it. It would settle the income question.

## 2. One number for the market read

**What exists.** Each morning the desk refreshes every watchlist name's implied volatility and where it sits in its own past-year range. "Rich" is the upper half of that range, the same bar the put list uses.

**The number, both ways**

| Morning | Sellable names (graded A–C or approved funds) | Whole watchlist |
|---|---|---|
| 5 Nov (before that day's fall) | 33 of 102 | 45 of 135 |
| 6 Nov | 70 of 102 | 88 of 135 |
| 12 Nov | 33 of 102 | 41 of 135 |
| 18 Nov | 64 of 102 | 80 of 135 |
| 20 Nov | 69 of 102 | 88 of 135 |

**Recommendation**
- **Report the sellable count:** "69 of 102". It is what the desk can act on. A third of the wider watchlist can't be sold under the desk's rules.
- **Where:** a small new block in the daily file the Book already reads, holding four things: the morning's date, names checked, names rich, and the bar (50). Nothing already in the file changes. The file is written three times a day and would carry the morning's figure each time; the date shows at once if a morning refresh was missed.
- **Optional:** the same count split into stocks and funds (this morning 51 of 78 and 18 of 24). The fund count mostly repeats what the Book's index readings already say.

**What the Book should know about this number**
- It says premium is dear against each name's own past year. It does not say premium is dear against how much the name is actually moving. The desk note of 20 November found the second is the better test.
- It rises in every selloff by construction, and it lags by a day: it read 33 on the morning of the 5 November fall and 70 the next morning.
- Three names have under a year of history behind their reading.

**Size of the job.** Small: one calculation from readings already stored, a new block in the file, and its tests. Nothing is built until Russ says go.
