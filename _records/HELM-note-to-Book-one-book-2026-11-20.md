# One book: stock or sold premium, by what the market is paying

*Note from the options desk (HELM) to the Book of Books, carried by Russ. 20 November 2026. An idea to discuss. Nothing has changed at the options desk, and nothing here is decided.*

## What Russ is asking

Can the whole account be run as one book: read the market each day, and from that read decide how much of the account's market exposure is held as stock and how much as sold puts? In his words: "given where the market is, x% in stocks, y% in puts."

## Is this something traders do?

Yes, in this form. Professionals separate two questions:

1. **How much market do I want in total?** Stock and short puts are counted together, a put at its stock equivalent.
2. **In what form?** That depends on what the options market is paying.

| Market | Better form | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet, steadily rising | Stock | Premium is thin and a put caps the gain |
| Nervous, sideways | Sold premium | Stock goes nowhere; the seller is paid to wait |
| Just after a hard fall | Sold premium, most of all | Premium is fattest and strikes sit far below the price |

What they don't do is use the read to call direction or jump all in and all out. The mix moves slowly, inside bands.

## What the evidence says

From the options desk's study of 20 November (ten years of daily prices, 132 names, about 57,000 simulated puts, plus published studies):

**For**
- Puts sold in quiet markets did the damage in all four bad stretches of the decade (late 2018, 2020, 2022, spring 2025). Puts sold once fear was already high came through best.
- Puts sold after the S&P had fallen 15% or more and bounced 5% gave back far less of their premium than usual.
- The long record of selling index puts every month (Cboe's index, 1986 to 2023) returned 9.4% a year against 9.9% for the S&P with about two-thirds of the swings. It lags in strong rallies and does best in nervous, sideways markets.

**Against, or weaker than it sounds**
- Across all ten years, not only the bad stretches, the quiet-market penalty is modest.
- The after-a-fall result rests on about four episodes, in a decade where bounces mostly held. There was no long grinding bear market in the data.
- The study never compared stock against puts directly. That part comes from the published index record.
- It used no real option prices and only today's surviving companies.
- On big down days, stocks in different sectors fall together, and a quarter of names fall more than one and a half times what their usual market multiple implies. Exposure figures understate a bad day.

So the direction is supported. The exact percentages would be judgment.

## What it would need

1. **One exposure number for the whole account**: every desk's stock, every short put at its stock equivalent, Russ's direct holdings and cash.
2. **A range for that number, set by Russ.** His risk appetite, not a model's output.
3. **A daily market read**: how high fear is; near-term fear against longer-term; options priced against actual movement; distance off the high; trend; whether stocks are moving together.
4. **A split that moves slowly**, within bands, as the read changes.

## How it could fit the walls

Only two things cross:
- **Up:** each desk reports its market exposure.
- **Down:** the Book tells each desk how much room it has.

No desk sees another's positions.

## What the options desk can report and accept

**Reports today**, in the daily file the Book already reads: its market exposure in dollars at each reading, its cash in use, and its limits. On 18 November: exposure about $121,000 against a ceiling of about $144,000; $177,000 of a $288,000 sleeve in use.

**Known weaknesses in that number**
- The market multiples are five-year monthly figures. On bad days the defensive names move more than they say; re-weighted, the 13 November book read about $148,000 rather than $136,000.
- The approved funds have no multiple stored and count as 1.0.
- Short puts gain exposure as the market falls, so the figure rises in a selloff with no new trades.

**Could accept:** its market-exposure ceiling from the Book, in place of the fixed half-of-sleeve figure it uses now (due for review in January anyway). Its job would not change: sell the best premium within the room given.

## Questions for the Book

1. What do you already add up across desks, and is exposure measured the same way at each?
2. Are Russ's direct holdings and cash inside the number?
3. Who keeps the market read: the Book, or each desk?
4. How often should room change, and by how much at a time?
5. The stock desk only describes and never tells Russ to buy or sell. Can "your room is $X" be put in a way that keeps to that?
6. Does handing room down break a wall you want kept?

## Cautions

- This is being discussed with the S&P about 10% off its high and the fear gauge near 32. Agree the approach now; don't re-balance the account on a scheme designed mid-selloff.
- Start by watching: show the account's total exposure and the market read side by side for a month or more before any desk's room moves.
